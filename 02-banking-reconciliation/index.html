Real scenarios this pipeline caught

Pulled directly from the live reconciliation output — not illustrative, these are actual rows.

1. The double-posted wire transfer

A wire transfer got entered into the internal ledger twice — same reference number, same account, same amount — likely a clerk re-running an import job without checking whether it had already loaded. Left alone, this would overstate the internal ledger's balance for that account by the full transaction amount.

TXN0005871 | Account 6225026093 | 2025-01-28 | Incoming Wire | -$27,243.16 → appears TWICE in the raw ledger export
How it was caught: dedupe_ledger() keys on (ref, account, amount, direction). The second occurrence is flagged and excluded before matching runs, so it can't silently double-count. 187 such duplicates were caught across the full dataset.

2. The wire that settled for the wrong amount

A $44,186.85 outgoing wire was recorded in the ledger, but the bank actually settled $199.50 less — $43,987.35. Formatting noise wouldn't explain a gap this size; something (a fee not passed through, a data entry error upstream, or a partial short-payment) actually happened here, and it's real money.

TXN0004844 | Account 1123836232 | 2025-05-01 Ledger: -$44,186.85 Bank: -$43,987.35 Diff: -$199.50
How it was caught: after normalizing both amounts to the same sign convention and decimal precision, any gap over $0.01 is classified Amount Discrepancy (Investigate) rather than silently accepted or silently dropped. This is exactly the kind of error a rounding-tolerant match would have missed.

3. The wire the bank never received

The ledger shows a $44,817.57 transaction posted and cleared internally — but no matching record exists anywhere in the bank's settlement feed. That's a real operational risk: either the payment never actually went out (and the counterparty is still waiting), or it's sitting somewhere in a batch that hasn't settled yet.

TXN0000683 | Account 1068910439 | 2025-02-02 | +$44,817.57 → no corresponding record in bank_statement_raw.csv
How it was caught: classified Ledger-Only (Investigate) — every ledger row that finds no match by cleaned reference + account after the full pass is surfaced, rather than being silently ignored because "the bank probably has it somewhere."

4. The bank transaction with no internal record

The inverse problem: the bank settled a $44,755.52 transaction that has zero trace in the internal ledger. If this turns out to not be a known fee, it needs to be booked — otherwise the bank's books and the internal books diverge quietly, and that gap compounds every reporting period until someone finds it.

TXN0000616 | Account 2155645245 | 2025-01-02 | -$44,755.52 → no corresponding record in internal_ledger_raw.csv, and ref doesn't match the FEE prefix used for known bank-originated charges
How it was caught: classified Bank-Only (Investigate) rather than lumped in with the 140 expected bank fees — the pipeline distinguishes "we know this is a fee" from "we have no idea what this is" instead of treating all unmatched bank items the same way.

5. The transaction that just took a few days (not an error)

Not every mismatch is a problem. A $44,906.16 transaction posted to the ledger on May 8th but didn't settle at the bank until May 10th — a completely normal in-transit lag for a wire clearing across systems. Flagging this as an "error" alongside the real discrepancies above would bury the signal in noise.

TXN0000525 | Account 5485544363 Ledger date: 2025-05-08 Bank date: 2025-05-10 (2 days later) Amount matches exactly on both sides
How it was caught: classified separately as Reconciled (Timing Difference) — same amount, different date, is treated as resolved with a note, not sent to anyone's investigation queue. This is the difference between a reconciliation process that's useful and one that trains people to ignore its output because it cries wolf.

How the pipeline actually works

  1. Defensive date parsing — the bank feed mixes MM/DD/YYYY and DD-Mon-YYYY depending on which upstream batch produced the row; the parser tries both rather than assuming one, which would silently misparse or crash on ~40% of rows.
  2. Account number normalization — strips all non-digit characters so 5685743966 and 568-574-3966 compare equal.
  3. Amount normalization — strips $ and commas, and correctly converts parenthesized accounting notation (($1,234.56)) to a negative value, the single most common gotcha in financial CSV exports.
  4. Deduplication — accidental double-postings in the ledger are detected and removed before matching, so they can't corrupt the reconciliation.
  5. Matching + classification — matches on cleaned reference + account number, then classifies every line into one of six categories (reconciled, timing difference, amount discrepancy, ledger-only, bank-only, expected fee) instead of a flat matched/unmatched binary that would bury the interesting cases.
