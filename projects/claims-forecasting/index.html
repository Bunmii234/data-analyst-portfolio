Focus: Technical Model

Claims Frequency Forecasting

Python NumPy Time-Series Decomposition Reserve Planning
The question this answers: how many claims — and how many claim dollars — should underwriting expect next month, so reserves get set before the fact instead of reacted to after? Built on the same insured-jewelry-logistics dataset as the dashboard project, joining claims back to their originating shipment to recover a claim date.

Method

Classical trend + seasonal decomposition, implemented directly with NumPy (no statsmodels/sklearn dependency) so every step is auditable rather than a black-box call:

  • Build a monthly claims-count time series, Jan 2024 – Jun 2026 (30 points)
  • Fit a linear trend via least squares (numpy.polyfit)
  • Compute a seasonal index per calendar month — the average ratio of actual to trend for that month across all years present
  • Forecast forward = trend projection × seasonal index
  • Build an 80% prediction interval from in-sample residual spread (normal approximation)
  • Convert the frequency forecast into a $ reserve forecast using historical average claim severity and the historical mix of claims across declared-value tiers

Honest limitation: 30 monthly observations is a thin base for estimating 12 separate seasonal indices — some months are backed by only 2-3 data points. The forecast is directionally useful (it correctly captures the Valentine's Day and holiday-season spikes baked into shipping volume) but the prediction intervals are wide, and that's stated rather than hidden.

Reserve dollars are allocated across declared-value tiers using the historical claims mix (Under $10K: 45.9%, $10K–$50K: 35.1%, $50K–$150K: 17.6%, $150K+: 1.4%) — so a $61.9K December reserve isn't a single number to hold, it's a tier-by-tier planning figure an underwriting team can actually act on.

Real scenarios this model addresses

Grounded in the actual numbers this model produced, not hypotheticals.

1. The reserve shortfall a flat average would have created

A simpler, common approach to reserving is "take the historical average and hold that every month." Across this dataset's history, that flat average is 2.47 claims/month, implying a reserve of roughly $44,790 for any given month. But December isn't an average month — it's a peak. The seasonal model forecasts 3.41 claims and $61,899.84 in reserve for December 2026, meaning a flat-average approach would have under-reserved December by $17,109.42 — money that needs to already be set aside before the claims arrive, not scrambled for afterward.

2. The February pattern that shows up every single year

February claims came in at 7 (2024), 6 (2025), and 4 (2026) — every single year meaningfully above the 2.47/month baseline, consistent with the Valentine's Day shipping surge built into the underlying shipment volume. That's not one lucky/unlucky month; it's the same pattern repeating three years running, which is exactly the kind of signal a seasonal index is supposed to catch (and why February carries the highest seasonal index in the model, at 2.50x baseline) rather than being treated as three unrelated coincidences.

3. Where the model would mislead if taken too literally

March tells a different story: 4 claims (2024), 3 claims (2025), then just 1 claim (2026) — a real drop that the model's fitted value (around 3.0) didn't see coming. With only three years of data behind each calendar month, a single unusually quiet or busy month swings the seasonal index a lot. That's exactly why this project ships prediction intervals instead of a single number, and why the write-up says outright that the intervals are wide rather than presenting false precision.

Skills demonstrated

Implementing a time-series decomposition from first principles (not just calling a library), being explicit about a model's sample-size limitations rather than overselling precision, and translating a statistical forecast into an operational figure (tier-allocated reserve $) that a non-technical stakeholder can use.

Data is synthetic, shared with the Insured Jewelry Logistics Dashboard project.
